Modelling how the ELT and OWL focus starlight

After a hands-on Zemax lab in my Physical Optics and Modeling course, I reconstructed ESO’s 39 m Extremely Large Telescope (ELT) and its proposed 100 m OverWhelmingly Large Telescope (OWL) in Zemax OpticStudio. The models follow the published optical prescriptions, including the one in ESO’s OWL Blue Book. I kept each design’s curved focal surface fixed and modelled point sources at five field angles from 0 to 5 arcmin. Using Python, NumPy and Matplotlib, I put the images at 550, 1650, 2200 and 4800 nm on a common angular scale. The models describe the nominal telescope optics and exclude atmospheric seeing and residual adaptive-optics errors. ESO ELT optical design · ESO OWL baseline design

At 5 arcmin off axis, my OWL model gave a total RMS wavefront error of 1.472 µm, within 0.3% of ESO’s 1.476 µm. At 3 arcmin and 2.2 µm, the model put 77.1% of the light inside a square with a half-width of 50 µm. I read about 49.9% from ESO’s curve, a difference of about 27 percentage points. With the radius fixed at 2215.4 mm, changing only the image surface from a hyperboloid to a sphere brought the fraction to 50.0%. The largest difference from my readings of ESO’s curve was then about 0.4 percentage points across the sampled half-widths. ESO does not specify which surface it used for that figure.

Half-light radii across the field

I measured the half-energy angular radius (EE50) for each telescope. A circle of this radius, centred on the image centroid, contains half the light. At 550 nm and 1 arcmin off axis, the radius is 0.80 milliarcseconds (mas) for OWL and 1.73 mas for the ELT. ESO specified a science field 3 arcmin in diameter for OWL, so this point lies inside the field. At its edge, 1.5 arcmin from the axis, the ELT radius is still 1.73 mas, while OWL’s has grown to 1.94 mas. OWL Blue Book, Table 6-1

A faint star with a bright neighbour

Suppose a faint star in a crowded part of a nearby galaxy has a bright neighbour. ESO gives a collecting area of 978 m² for the ELT and roughly 7,000 m² for the proposed OWL. In the same band, with equal throughput and exposure time, OWL would collect about 7.2 times as many photons from the faint star. For stars of similar colour, OWL would record about as many photons from a V = 26.1 star as the ELT would from a V = 24.0 star, a 2.1-magnitude difference. ESO’s ELT facts · OWL Blue Book, §4.2.1

For a star 1.5′ off axis, the comparison depends on wavelength. At 2.2 µm, half its light falls within a radius of 2.93 mas in my OWL model and 6.67 mas in the ELT model. The OWL circle covers only 19% of the angular area of the ELT circle. At 550 nm, the ELT has the smaller half-energy radius, 1.73 mas compared with 1.94 mas for OWL. How faint a star I could measure in a crowded field would also depend on the sky background, the quality of the adaptive-optics correction and how much light from the bright neighbour falls inside the circle. Resolving individual stars in other galaxies was one of ESO’s science goals for OWL. OWL Blue Book, §2.5

For the 550 nm measurements, I kept each telescope’s design focal surface and on-axis focus. In DENC, I selected Huygens diffraction, a centroid reference (Refp 4), a circle (Type 1) and an energy fraction of 0.5. The Value column then gives the radius, in µm, of the circle enclosing half the light. I used 256 × 256 pupil sampling (Pupil Samp 4) and a 512 × 512 image grid (I Samp 5). The image spacing (I Delta) was 0.829058 µm for the ELT and 0.730087 µm for OWL.

I converted each radius to mas before subtracting the ELT value from the OWL value. The plate scales were 0.30154722 mas/µm for the ELT and 0.34242503 mas/µm for OWL. I transcribed the DENC values at the time, but did not record the exact model revision for each row.

Matching ESO’s ensquared-energy curve at 3′

My on-axis and 1′ curves at 2.2 µm were close to ESO’s. At 3′, my OWL model put 77.4% of the light in a square of half-width 50 µm, while I read about 50% from ESO’s yellow curve. I checked the box size and the field angle first. Both plots measure from the centroid to the edge, so the square is 100 µm across. The Zemax field label, 0.0500°, corresponds to 3′.

ESO gives a focal-surface radius of 2209.788 mm in the optical prescription and 2215.4 mm in the table for its 2.2 µm Strehl field. Changing only the radius to the second value reduced the ensquared energy from 53.4% to 44.3% at a half-width of 25 µm. At 50 µm, the change was much smaller, from 77.4% to 77.1%.

The IMAGE row in Zemax also specifies the conic constant. Surfaces with the same curvature on axis can differ in height farther out. A Blue Book footnote mentions placing wavefront sensors on a sphere, so I kept the radius at 2215.4 mm and tested a sphere (K = 0) in place of the prescribed hyperboloid (K = −1.295298). Before rerunning Zemax, I calculated the ensquared energy in Python. The prediction for the sphere was 13.4% at a half-width of 25 µm and 49.7% at 50 µm.

Point-source images at the edge of OWL’s science field

At 550 nm and 1.5 arcmin off axis, OWL’s diffraction rings are asymmetric around a narrow central peak. The Strehl ratio compares the peak intensity with that of the same telescope free of aberrations. For OWL it falls from 0.889 at 1 arcmin to 0.556 at 1.5 arcmin. For the ELT it is 0.994 at 1.5 arcmin.

At 2.2 µm and the same field angle (1.5 arcmin), the point-spread function (PSF) from Zemax gives OWL a Strehl ratio of 0.964. The corresponding half-light radii are 2.93 mas for OWL and 6.67 mas for the ELT (Fig. 1a). In both the tangential and sagittal directions, the modulation transfer function (MTF) falls to 50% contrast at about 57 cycles per arcsecond for OWL and 27 for the ELT.

How OWL’s wavefront changes across the field

I fitted Standard Zernike polynomials to OWL’s wavefront on its curved focal surface at 2.2 µm. At 1.5 arcmin, the largest terms among Z4–Z22 were secondary spherical aberration, primary trefoil and defocus, each about 0.010–0.011 waves in magnitude. At 5 arcmin, well outside the science field, primary trefoil and primary coma dominated the fit, with magnitudes of 0.409 waves and 0.295 waves, respectively.

Choosing a focal surface for OWL

All three surfaces gave the same on-axis spot size, so I’d start with a flat detector for a small patch around the axis. By 1.5′, at the edge of OWL’s science field, the spot on the flat plane had broadened. The spot sizes on the two curved surfaces were still nearly identical. The 2.2 µm exports used for Fig. 1c also include 1.5′. There, both curved surfaces put about 92% of the light inside a square of half-width 50 µm. For an instrument covering that field, I’d try the simpler spherical surface first.

At 3′, the edge of the wavefront-sensing field, the spot is visibly wider on the sphere. In the diffraction calculation at this field angle, a square of half-width 50 µm encloses half the light on the sphere and 77% on the hyperboloid (Fig. 1c). For a wavefront sensor at that field angle, I’d follow the hyperboloid in the prescription.
