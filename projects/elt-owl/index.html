How far off-axis does OWL retain its aperture advantage over the ELT?

A 100 m telescope can concentrate starlight into a smaller angle than a 39 m telescope. How much of that advantage survives away from the centre of the field? In my optical models, the answer depends on wavelength. At the edge of OWL’s specified science field, the ELT encloses half the light in a smaller angular radius at 550 nm. At 2.2 µm, OWL needs only 44% of the ELT’s radius.

I started this personal project after the hands-on Zemax lab in my Physical Optics and Modeling course. I reconstructed ESO’s 39 m Extremely Large Telescope (ELT) and its proposed 100 m OverWhelmingly Large Telescope (OWL) in Zemax OpticStudio from published optical prescriptions. I kept each design on its own fixed curved focal surface and compared point sources at five off-axis angles from 0 to 5 arcmin, at wavelengths of 550, 1650, 2200 and 4800 nm. I used Python, NumPy and Matplotlib to put both telescopes on the same angular scale. The results describe the nominal telescope optics before atmospheric seeing and residual adaptive-optics errors are included. ESO ELT optical design · ESO OWL baseline design

Where the two designs change places

I used the half-energy angular radius (EE50) to ask how small a circle around the image centroid contains half the light. At 550 nm and 1 arcmin off-axis, OWL needs a radius of just 0.80 milliarcseconds (mas), compared with 1.73 mas for the ELT. At 1.5 arcmin, the ELT is still at 1.73 mas, while OWL has grown to 1.94 mas. The location matters because ESO specified a 3 arcmin diameter science field for OWL. The 1 arcmin point is inside it, and 1.5 arcmin is its edge. OWL Blue Book, Table 6-1

At 550 nm, I kept each telescope’s design focal surface and on-axis focus. In DENC I selected Huygens with the image centroid as the reference (Refp 4), a circle (Type 1) and an energy fraction of 0.5. With those settings, the Value column gives EE50 directly in µm. It is the radius of the circle enclosing half the light. Pupil Samp was 4 (256 × 256). I used a 512 × 512 image grid (I Samp 5) with I Delta of 0.829058 µm for ELT and 0.730087 µm for OWL.

The tabulated difference uses the two angular scales. For ELT, multiply the radius in µm by 0.30154722 mas/µm. For OWL, use 0.34242503 mas/µm. I transcribed the DENC values at the time. The exact model revision for each row was not recorded.

The 40-point CSV also gives the infrared comparison. At 2.2 µm and 1.5′, OWL / ELT = 2.9318 / 6.6696 = 43.96%. Ansys · DENC

Why was my 3′ result so different from ESO’s?

At 2.2 µm, my OWL curve gave 77.4% at 3′ and ESO’s yellow curve gave about 50%. I wondered if I’d read the width of the box wrong. Both plots measure from the centroid to the edge, so 50 µm means a square 100 µm across. I checked the field label too: 0.0500° is 3′. The on-axis and 1′ curves agreed quite closely. It was the 3′ curve I still had to explain.

The first thing I changed was the focal-surface radius. I’d taken 2209.788 mm from ESO’s prescription, but found 2215.4 mm in the table for its 2.2 µm Strehl field. I tried the second value and left the rest of the model alone. In the smaller, 25 µm half-width box, the fraction fell from 53.4% to 44.3%. That looked promising until I checked the 50 µm box. It still held 77.1%, barely down from 77.4%.

I went back to the IMAGE row in Zemax. Next to the radius was another setting I’d kept from the prescription: Conic. Two surfaces can have the same curvature on axis and still sit at different heights farther out. A footnote in the Blue Book mentions placing wavefront sensors on a sphere. That gave me something specific to try: keep the 2215.4 mm radius and swap the prescribed hyperboloid (K = −1.295298) for a sphere (K = 0). Before running Zemax again, I tried it in Python. I got 13.4% at 25 µm and 49.7% at 50 µm.

What a star looks like at the science-field edge

At 550 nm, OWL’s image still has a narrow central peak at 1.5 arcmin, but its diffraction rings are visibly asymmetric. Its Strehl ratio, which compares the peak intensity with that of its own aberration-free reference, falls from 0.889 at 1 arcmin to 0.556 at 1.5 arcmin. The ELT gives 0.994 at the same edge position.

At 2.2 µm and 1.5 arcmin, the OWL PSF export gives a Strehl ratio of 0.964. Figure 1a gives the EE50 radii at that field, 2.93 mas for OWL and 6.67 mas for the ELT. From the MTF curves I read the spatial frequency at 50% contrast. In both tangential and sagittal directions it is about 57 cycles per arcsecond for OWL and 27 for the ELT.

Which aberrations grow off-axis?

I fitted Standard Zernike polynomials to OWL’s wavefront on its curved focal surface at 2.2 µm. At 1.5 arcmin, the largest fitted terms among Z4–Z22 were secondary spherical aberration, primary trefoil and defocus, each about 0.010–0.011 waves in magnitude. Farther out, their relative importance changed. At 5 arcmin, well outside the science field, primary trefoil reached 0.409 waves and primary coma 0.295 waves. The aberrations that dominate the outer field are already distinguishable from those at the science-field edge.

ESO original · OWL baseline design

At 5 arcmin off-axis, the edge of OWL’s full optical field, I calculated a total RMS wavefront error of approximately 1.472 µm from my centroid-referenced Zemax field scan. ESO reports 1.476 µm for its baseline design in Table 6-6. My result is about 4 nm lower, or 0.3% relative to ESO’s value. This comparison tests the total wavefront error at one field position. What happens when I change OWL’s focal surface?

I kept the mirrors and the on-axis focus fixed and ran the same 2.2 µm ray trace on three receiving surfaces. I used a tangent plane, a sphere (K = 0) and a hyperboloid (K = −1.295298). The two curved surfaces share the 2215.4 mm vertex radius I tested in Fig. 1c. This time I followed the spot from the axis all the way out to 5′.

Which focal surface would I use?

I’d start with a flat detector if I only needed a small patch around the optical axis. All three surfaces gave me the same spot size at the centre. By 1.5′, the edge of OWL’s science field, the spot on the flat plane had spread out, while the two curved surfaces still gave almost identical spots. The 2.2 µm Zemax exports I used for Fig. 1c also include the 1.5′ field. There, both surfaces put about 92% of the light inside a square with a 50 µm half-width. If my instrument only went out to 1.5′, I’d try the simpler spherical surface first.

At 3′, the edge of the wavefront-sensing field, what caught my eye was how much wider the spot looked on the sphere. At this field, the 50 µm half-width readings in Fig. 1c put the difference in terms of light inside the square. The sphere caught half the light and the hyperboloid caught 77%. If I were placing a wavefront sensor that far off axis, I’d follow the hyperboloid in the prescription.

与 ELT 相比，OWL 的大口径优势能保持到多大的离轴角？

100 米望远镜能把星光集中到比 39 米望远镜更小的角范围内。把恒星从视场中心移向边缘，这个优势还能保留多少？在我重建的光学模型中，答案取决于波长。到达 OWL 设计科学视场的边缘时，550 nm 下已经是 ELT 能把一半星光集中在更小的角半径内。换到 2.2 µm，OWL 所需的半径却只有 ELT 的 44%。

上完 Physical Optics and Modeling 课里的 Zemax 上机实验后，我出于个人兴趣做了这个项目。我根据 ESO 公开的光学处方，在 Zemax OpticStudio 中重建了 39 米极大望远镜 ELT 和 100 米 OWL 概念设计。我将两台望远镜分别固定在各自的设计曲面焦面上，在 0–5′ 范围内选取五个场点，计算 550、1650、2200 和 4800 nm 四个波长的成像结果，再用 Python、NumPy 和 Matplotlib 将它们画在相同的天空角尺度上。这里考察的是望远镜的名义光学性能，大气视宁度和自适应光学残差属于后续端到端模拟的范围。ESO 的 ELT 光学设计 · ESO 的 OWL 基准设计

我在 3′ 算出的结果为什么和 ESO 差这么多？

在 2.2 µm、离轴 3′ 处，我的 OWL 曲线读出来是 77.4%，ESO 的黄线却只有约 50%。我先怀疑自己是不是把框的宽度看错了。两张图的横轴都是从质心量到框边，所以半宽 50 µm，框的边长就是 100 µm。我又对了一遍场点，图例里的 0.0500° 确实是 3′。轴上和 1′ 的曲线也都挺接近，偏偏是 3′ 对不上。

我先试了焦面半径，因为我用的 2209.788 mm 是从 ESO 处方里抄的，但它在 2.2 µm 的 Strehl 视场表里又写了 2215.4 mm。我换上第二个值，其余设置照旧。看半宽 25 µm 的小框，能量从 53.4% 降到了 44.3%，我以为找对了。再看原来对不上的 50 µm，里面还有 77.1% 的光，和之前的 77.4% 几乎一样。

我又回到 Zemax 的 IMAGE 那一行。除了半径，还有一项我一直照抄处方的参数：Conic，也就是圆锥常数。两个面在轴心可以弯得一样，到了边上却不一定在同一个高度。刚好蓝皮书有个脚注，提到可以沿球面放置波前传感器。我就想，半径还是 2215.4 mm，只把处方双曲面（K = −1.295298）换成球面（K = 0），会怎么样？我先在 Python 里算了一遍，25 和 50 µm 处分别得到 13.4% 和 49.7%。

科学视场边缘的一颗星，看起来是什么样？

在 550 nm、离轴 1.5′ 处，OWL 的星像仍有窄小的中心峰，但周围的衍射环已明显不对称。Strehl 比衡量实际峰值相对于本系统无像差参考峰值的比例。OWL 的 Strehl 从 1′ 处的 0.889 降至 1.5′ 处的 0.556，而 ELT 在 1.5′ 处仍为 0.994。

在 2.2 µm、离轴 1.5′ 处，OWL 的 PSF 导出给出的 Strehl 比为 0.964。图 1a 中这个场点的 EE50 半径，OWL 是 2.93 mas，ELT 是 6.67 mas。我从 MTF 曲线上读出对比度降到 50% 时的空间频率。切向和弧矢两个方向上，OWL 都约为 57 周期/角秒，ELT 都约为 27 周期/角秒。

哪些像差随离轴角增大？

我在 OWL 的设计曲面焦面上，对 2.2 µm 的波前做了 Standard Zernike 分解。在 1.5′ 处，Z4–Z22 中幅度最大的三项是次级球差、初级三叶和离焦，系数绝对值都在 0.010–0.011 波左右。继续移到科学视场以外，像差组成发生了变化。到 5′ 时，初级三叶达到 0.409 波，初级彗差达到 0.295 波，成为幅度最大的两项。因此，解释科学视场边缘的星像时，需要看该位置的波前；直接套用 5′ 的像差排序会遗漏次级球差和离焦在边缘的相对重要性。

这三种焦面，我会怎么选？

如果只用光轴附近一小块地方，我会先试平面探测器。正中心的光斑，三种焦面算出来一样大。可到了 OWL 科学视场的边缘 1.5′，平面上的光斑已经散开了，球面和双曲面上的还差不多大。图 1c 用的那两份 2.2 µm 方框能量导出里也有 1.5′ 这个场点。看半宽 50 µm 那一行，两种曲面都收进了大约 92% 的光。仪器如果只用到这里，我会先试形状更简单的球面。

到了 3′，也就是波前传感视场的边缘，两张点列图放在一起，我最先注意到的是球面上的光斑横向摊开了。图 1c 里半宽 50 µm 的读数说的就是这个位置。球面上，框里只收进一半的光，双曲面上还能收进 77%。如果要把波前传感器放到这么靠外的位置，我会按处方里的双曲面来定位。
